What Is Benign Prostatic Hyperplasia (BPH) and Why Does It Happen?

A non-cancerous growth of the prostate gland that gradually presses on the urethra and slows down urine flow — that's benign prostatic hyperplasia in plain terms. It isn't cancer, but once it starts interfering with daily life, it needs a proper look from a specialist.

Doctors sometimes use the term prostatomegaly when describing a prostate that's grown past its normal size. In the early stages, mild prostatomegaly can pass with barely any noticeable symptoms, which is exactly why so many men don't catch it until it's more advanced.

Put simply, BPH meaning comes down to this: the prostate expands beyond its usual volume without turning cancerous. This second wave of prostate gland enlargement typically kicks off after age 25 and builds slowly, driven mainly by shifting testosterone and estrogen levels. Knowing why prostate enlargement happens is what pushes men to get checked sooner instead of waiting for things to get worse.

Recognising Symptoms of Prostate Enlargement in Men

As prostate enlargement progresses, it puts steadily increasing pressure on the bladder and urethra — and that pressure is what shows up as urinary symptoms over time.

Symptoms typically follow a grading pattern:

  • Grade 1 prostate enlargement — mildly enlarged, a slight dip in urine flow
  • Grade 2 prostate enlargement — noticeable frequency, weak stream, or hesitancy starting a stream
  • Moderate prostatomegaly — trouble emptying the bladder fully, frequent nighttime urination
  • Straining to start, or a stream that stops and starts

A urologist confirms the exact BPH prostate grade through ultrasound imaging combined with a urinary flow test — not guesswork based on symptoms alone.

Is prostate enlargement normal as men get older? It's common, yes — but common isn't the same as something you should just live with once it starts affecting your daily routine.

Understanding Prostate Cancer — Signs, Diagnosis & Treatment

Prostate cancer is the most commonly diagnosed cancer in men, and it often grows for years without any obvious symptoms. Most men feel nothing in the early stage — which is exactly why warning signs of prostate cancer shouldn't be the thing you wait for.

Early Signs Difficulty urinating, a weak stream, blood in semen — signs of prostate cancer are easy to dismiss as "just age"
Risk Factors Age over 50, family history, a high-fat diet, and a sedentary lifestyle raise your risk
Diagnosis Starts with a PSA blood test, followed by a Gleason score to grade aggressiveness, then imaging or biopsy to confirm

A prostate cancer diagnosis is never based on one test alone — prostate cancer screening and a PSA reading work together to confirm what's actually going on.

Is prostate cancer curable when caught early? Yes. Localised prostate cancer treatment at Stage I or II carries a 5-year survival rate above 95%, and your prognosis ultimately comes down to how early it's caught and how well it responds to that first round of care.

Kidney Stones — From First Symptom to the Right Treatment

A kidney stone forms when minerals like calcium, oxalate, or uric acid get too concentrated in urine and harden into a solid mass. Sizes vary, but the pain when one starts moving through the urinary tract rarely does — it's sharp.

01

Step 1 — Spotting the Pattern

Men develop kidney stones more often than women, and dehydration, a high-salt diet, and excess weight all raise the risk. Kidney stones and frequent urination often get mistaken for a prostate issue — only imaging tells the two apart.

02

Step 2 — Confirming It's Not Something Else

Persistent kidney disease frequent urination shouldn't be brushed off. An ultrasound or CT scan confirms whether a stone, and not the prostate, is behind what you're feeling.

03

Step 3 — Deciding If It Needs Intervention

Stones under 5mm often pass on their own with hydration and pain management. Kidney stone surgery becomes necessary once a stone is too large to pass or is causing a blockage, and a kidney stone operation is urgent if infection, bleeding, or kidney function is at risk.

04

Step 4 — Choosing the Right Procedure

RIRS kidney stone — a flexible scope through the urethra, treated with laser
PCNL kidney stone — a small back puncture for direct access to larger stones
ESWL kidney stone — external sound waves break the stone into passable pieces

Kidney stone laser treatment is now the preferred route for most cases — precise, minimally invasive, and it keeps stone removal out of open surgery almost every time. Recovery after kidney stone laser surgery typically runs 1–3 days for RIRS and ESWL, and 3–5 days for PCNL, depending on stone size and location.

Frequent Urination in Men — Causes & Treatment

Going more than 8 times in 24 hours, or waking up repeatedly at night to use the bathroom, is what doctors call frequent urination — and it's one of the most overlooked symptoms in male urology. Nocturia is often the first real warning sign, showing up long before daytime urgency does.

The causes of frequent urination in men vary widely. Prostate problems are the most common trigger past 50, though a urinary tract infection, diabetes, or an overactive bladder can just as easily be behind it. Even younger men aren't immune — frequent urination in older men gets most of the attention, but it isn't exclusive to that group.

Treatment depends entirely on what's driving it:

Medication & Bladder Retraining

Targeted drugs plus scheduled bathroom timing to regain control gradually.

Managing the Root Cause

Treating enlarged prostate frequent urination, a UTI, or blood sugar issues directly, not just the symptom.

Lifestyle & Fluid Timing

Adjusting when and how much you drink, especially in the evening, to cut down on frequent nighttime urination.

Specialist Evaluation

Recommended if you're going more than 8 times daily or waking twice or more at night.

Frequent urination treatment only works once the underlying cause is identified — guessing rarely fixes it for good.

Bladder Infection in Men — When It's More Than Just an Infection

Less common in men than in women, a bladder infection carries more weight when it does show up — it often points to something else going on underneath, not just bad luck.

You should book a visit if:

  • Burning during urination or cloudy, foul-smelling urine
  • A persistent urge to go, paired with lower abdominal pressure or pelvic discomfort
  • Recurrent bladder infections happening more than twice a year
  • Symptoms not responding to antibiotics after a full course

Bladder cystitis — inflammation of the bladder lining, usually from bacterial infection — is the clinical term behind most of these symptoms. But unlike women, where a simple UTI is common and low-risk, a urinary bladder infection in men often signals an enlarged prostate trapping urine, a kidney stone, or a urinary obstruction underneath it.

A chronic bladder infection in men that keeps returning needs a full workup — cystoscopy and urine culture — not another round of the same antibiotic. Bladder infection treatment works best when it targets what's letting the bacteria in, not just the flare-up in front of you.

Can Bladder Cancer Be Treated Successfully?

BBlood in the urine is usually the first of the bladder cancer symptoms — and because it's often painless, many men mistake it for a minor issue and let weeks slip by before getting it checked.

Bladder cancer in men shows up three to four times more often than in women, with smoking responsible for nearly half of all cases. Urothelial carcinoma is the most frequent type, starting in the cells lining the bladder wall, though types of bladder cancer also include squamous cell and adenocarcinoma variants — each treated differently. Bladder cancer causes are well documented: smoking, chemical exposure, and chronic bladder inflammation top the list, and awareness early on genuinely lowers risk.

Bladder cancer cystoscopy remains the gold standard for diagnosis — a thin camera gives a direct look inside the bladder. From there, bladder cancer staging ranges from non-muscle-invasive disease to muscle-invasive and metastatic cases, which is what ultimately shapes the treatment plan.

Is bladder cancer curable? At Stage I or II, yes — with the right surgical and medical approach, long-term remission is very achievable. The bladder cancer survival rate for non-muscle-invasive disease caught early exceeds 90% at 5 years.
